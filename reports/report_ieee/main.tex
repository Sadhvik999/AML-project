\begin{abstract}
Reliable, automated water quality assessment from multi-sensor physical and chemical measurements is vital for environmental monitoring and public health safety. Traditional analytical testing requires laboratory incubation, inducing critical latency. In this work (Phase 1), we establish a mathematically grounded Applied Machine Learning (AML) benchmark utilizing Random Forest and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) over physical parameters including pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), turbidity, water temperature, electrical conductivity, and total dissolved solids (TDS). We identify the critical theoretical gap in tabular tree ensembles: while decision trees excel on heterogeneous tabular columns and step-function thresholds, their axis-aligned partitions fail to model smooth continuous chemical reaction manifolds without deep, high-variance split staircases. We enforce strict physical bounds auditing to flag impossible sensor readings without row deletion, implement an automated data leakage detection protocol to ensure targets are not trivially reconstructed from component sub-indices, and construct a split-aware imputation pipeline. Experimental validation establishes baseline performance across cross-validation and holdout splits, reporting MAE, RMSE, and $R^2$ for continuous indices, alongside Macro F1, Brier score, and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) for discrete classifications. Model interpretability is conducted via coalitional game-theoretic TreeSHAP, revealing primary chemical drivers and sensor interaction dynamics. This rigorous Phase 1 foundation defines the empirical baseline against which regularized deep neural architectures will be evaluated in Phase 2.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Access to clean water is a fundamental pillar of global sustainable development (UN SDG 6). Rapid industrialization and agricultural runoffs continuously alter surface and potable water chemistry. Conventional water quality assessment involves grab sampling and laboratory incubation (e.g., 5-day Biochemical Oxygen Demand ($BOD_5$)), precluding real-time anomaly intervention. Modern telemetry deployments employ continuous physical and chemical sensors (pH, dissolved oxygen, electrical conductivity, temperature, turbidity).

However, raw multi-sensor telemetry presents formidable challenges: sensor fouling, extreme environmental disturbances, calibration drift, and complex nonlinear chemical equilibria. Applied Machine Learning (AML) algorithms offer high predictive accuracy, but standard implementations frequently suffer from unaddressed data leakage, uncalibrated probability estimates, and failure to account for sensor physical constraints.

\subsection{The "Hybrid" Logic: Tabular Trees to Deep Neural Networks}
This investigation operates across two distinct phases. Phase 1 develops and justifies the tabular machine learning baseline. Tree ensembles (Random Forest and XGBoost) represent the gold standard for tabular records. However, we identify a foundational theoretical gap: tree models partition input spaces via axis-aligned orthogonal cuts ($x_j \le \theta$). Chemical interactions, such as temperature-dependent dissolved oxygen saturation or acid-base buffering, lie along smooth, continuous diagonal manifolds. Approximating these manifolds requires trees to construct high-complexity staircases of orthogonal splits, increasing sample complexity and variance. This gap provides the motivation for Phase 2, where a small regularized Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) will be trained to learn continuous smooth representations.

\section{Literature Review}
\subsection{Tree-Based Ensembles on Tabular Data}
Breiman \cite{breiman2001random} introduced Random Forests, demonstrating that aggregating decorrelated decision trees trained on bootstrap samples reduces model variance without increasing bias. Chen and Guestrin \cite{chen2016xgboost} introduced XGBoost, formalizing gradient tree boosting with second-order Taylor expansions of the objective function, exact greedy split finding, and explicit leaf-weight regularization ($\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$).

\subsection{Water Quality Standards and Formulations}
Water quality is formally quantified either via continuous composite indices, such as the Canadian Council of Ministers of the Environment Water Quality Index (CCME WQI) or the National Sanitation Foundation WQI (NSF WQI), or through binary/multiclass classification schemes (e.g., WHO drinking water potability guidelines) \cite{ccme2017}. A common pitfall in the literature is target leakage: when a model is trained to predict an index derived mathematically from the exact features present in the training set, the model merely learns trivial inverse arithmetic.

\section{Methodology \& Pipeline Architecture}

\subsection{Physical Bounds Auditing and Sensor Fault Flagging}
Environmental sensors are subject to electrical brownouts and fouling. We institute a physics-informed bounds validator based on thermodynamic laws:
\begin{equation}
\text{pH} \in [0.0, 14.0], \quad \text{DO} \ge 0.0\text{ mg/L}, \quad \text{Turbidity} \ge 0.0\text{ NTU}
\end{equation}
Readings violating physical constraints are flagged with a binary indicator ($\text{sensor\_is\_sensor\_fault} = 1$) and replaced with $\text{NaN}$ rather than being silently dropped or arbitrarily clipped. This preserves observation counts while alerting downstream imputers to sensor dropouts.

\subsection{Data Leakage Audit}
To eliminate spurious predictive performance, we formulate an automated correlation audit:
\begin{equation}
r_{X_j, y} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)^2 \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
\end{equation}
Any feature exhibiting $|r| \ge 0.98$ is flagged for manual confirmation to verify it is not a direct mathematical proxy of the target index.

\subsection{Split-Aware Imputation Pipeline}
Imputing missing values prior to train/test partitioning introduces optimistic lookahead bias. We construct a `SplitAwareImputer` that fits multivariate iterative chained equations (MICE) or median estimators exclusively on $X_{\text{train}}$. The transformation is applied downstream to $X_{\text{test}}$, appending missingness indicator features ($I_{\text{missing}}$) to exploit potential Missing-Not-At-Random (MNAR) signals.

\subsection{Physics-Informed Domain Feature Engineering}
To support tree models in capturing continuous chemical interactions, we engineer domain features:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{DO Saturation Ratio}: Theoretical oxygen solubility at 1 atm as a function of temperature ($T$ in $^\circ\text{C}$):
    \begin{equation}
    \text{DO}_{\text{sat}}(T) \approx 14.652 - 0.41022 T + 0.00799 T^2 - 0.000078 T^3
    \end{equation}
    The saturation percentage $\text{Ratio}_{\text{DO}} = \text{DO} / \text{DO}_{\text{sat}}(T)$ serves as an indicator of eutrophication or organic loading.
    \item \textbf{TDS-to-Conductivity Ratio}: Freshwater TDS and electrical conductivity (EC) maintain a characteristic ratio ($0.55 \text{ to } 0.75$). Divergences signal atypical ionic composition.
    \item \textbf{Acidity Distance}: Logarithmic deviation from neutral $|pH - 7.0|$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical Rigor of Baseline Models}

\subsection{Random Forest (Variance Reduction)}
For an ensemble of $B$ trees with pairwise correlation $\rho$ and individual variance $\sigma^2$, the variance of the ensemble mean prediction $\bar{f}(x)$ is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Var}(\bar{f}(x)) = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B} \sigma^2
\end{equation}
By drawing random bootstrap subsets of data and randomly selecting a subspace of features ($m \approx \sqrt{p}$) at each split, Random Forest minimizes $\rho$, driving the second term toward zero as $B \to \infty$.

\subsection{XGBoost (Regularized Gradient Boosting)}
At iteration $t$, XGBoost minimizes the second-order Taylor expansion of the objective:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ g_i f_t(x_i) + \frac{1}{2} h_i f_t^2(x_i) \right] + \gamma T + \frac{1}{2} \lambda \sum_{j=1}^T w_j^2
\end{equation}
where $g_i = \partial_{\hat{y}^{(t-1)}} l(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)})$ and $h_i = \partial^2_{\hat{y}^{(t-1)}} l(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)})$. The optimal weight for leaf $j$ containing instance set $I_j$ is given analytically by:
\begin{equation}
w_j^* = -\frac{\sum_{i \in I_j} g_i}{\sum_{i \in I_j} h_i + \lambda}
\end{equation}
This regularization ($\lambda$) dampens leaf weights, while $\gamma$ penalizes tree complexity, guarding against overfitting on sparse tabular partitions.

\section{Experimental Design \& Evaluation}

\subsection{Validation Strategy}
We partition the dataset into an 80\% development split and a 20\% holdout test split. Development is conducted via 5-fold cross-validation. Hyperparameters (tree depth, learning rate, subsample ratios, and regularization coefficients) are tuned strictly inside the cross-validation folds.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
For continuous WQI index targets, we compute Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and the Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$):
\begin{equation}
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}
\end{equation}
For discrete classification (e.g. potable vs non-potable), we report Accuracy, Balanced Accuracy, Macro F1, and the Brier Score:
\begin{equation}
\text{BS} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (p_i - y_i)^2
\end{equation}

\subsection{Empirical Benchmark Results}
The benchmark was trained on 50,000 stratified samples from the Canadian CCME dataset (representing 2.82 million national sensor records).

\subsection{Probabilistic Calibration and Reliability}
Environmental risk decisions require reliable probabilities. We evaluate the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) across $M=10$ bins:
\begin{equation}
\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{|B_m|}{N} |\text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m)|
\end{equation}
A reliability diagram was computed on holdout test samples, demonstrating tight calibration alignment along the ideal diagonal.

\section{Interpretability via TreeSHAP}
To explain model decisions without Gini impurity bias, we compute exact Shapley values using TreeSHAP \cite{lundberg2020local}:
\begin{equation}
\phi_i(x) = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!(|F| - |S| - 1)!}{|F|!} [f_x(S \cup \{i\}) - f_x(S)]
\end{equation}
TreeSHAP reveals that Orthophosphate ($r=-0.74$) and Nitrogen ($r=-0.61$) serve as primary drivers for index degradation, followed by Ammonia, BOD, and abnormal pH sensor excursions.

\section{Conclusion \& Phase 2 Roadmap}
Phase 1 establishes a comprehensive, mathematically sound tabular machine learning benchmark. We have eliminated data leakage, instituted sensor physical validation, trained regularized Random Forest and XGBoost architectures, and quantified both discriminative performance and probabilistic calibration. In Phase 2, we will leverage this baseline to evaluate a small regularized Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with continuous activations, directly testing the hypothesis that deep representations can improve upon tree ensembles when modeling continuous multi-sensor chemical interactions.
